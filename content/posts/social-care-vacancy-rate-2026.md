In June 2026, a headline landed that most care managers had waited a decade to read.

The adult social care vacancy rate in England had fallen to its lowest level in ten years.

It is tempting to stop there. Pour a coffee. Take the win.

But good news in a dataset is a bit like a calm sea. It tells you about the surface. It says very little about the current underneath. And the current in social care is pulling in a direction that should worry every employer, every commissioner, and every person who will one day need care.

Let's look at what the numbers actually say.

## The headline: social care vacancies at a 10-year low

According to Skills for Care's latest [size and structure report](https://www.skillsforcare.org.uk/Adult-Social-Care-Workforce-Data/workforceintelligence/resources/Reports/National/The-size-and-structure-of-the-adult-social-care-sector-and-workforce-in-England-2026.pdf), the vacancy rate in adult social care fell to **6.2% in 2025/26**. That is roughly 96,000 unfilled roles on any given day, and 10.5% fewer than the year before.

For context, the rate peaked at 10.4% in March 2022, when around 150,000 roles sat empty in the wake of the pandemic, as [Community Care reported](https://www.communitycare.co.uk/content/news/adult-social-care-vacancy-rate-falls-to-lowest-level-in-a-decade).

The workforce also grew for a fourth year in a row, adding about 22,000 filled posts.

That's real progress. Employers worked hard for it.

Now for the part that doesn't fit on a banner.

## Problem one: growth is slowing

That 22,000 increase works out at 1.4%. It is the slowest growth rate in four years.

A slowing growth rate matters because demand is not slowing. England's population is ageing, and Skills for Care estimates the sector will need **410,000 additional posts by 2040**.

Think of it like filling a bath with the plug half out. The water level rose this year. But the tap is being turned down while the bath keeps getting bigger.

## Problem two: the British workforce is shrinking

This is the number that deserves more attention than it gets.

Posts filled by people with British nationality **fell by 40,000 in a single year**, according to [National Health Executive's summary of the report](https://www.nationalhealthexecutive.com/articles/adult-social-care-vacancy-rate-falls-decade-low-challenges-persist). Since 2020/21, the drop is around 130,000.

For the past three years, the gap was covered by international recruitment. Community Care noted that non-British, non-EU staff rose to an estimated 430,000 in 2025/26.

In other words, the sector did not solve its domestic recruitment problem. It routed around it.

## Problem three: the international route has closed

New overseas sponsorship for care worker and senior care worker roles closed on 22 July 2025. People already in the UK on those visas can extend or switch employer under transitional rules, but the door for new arrivals from abroad is shut.

The effect is already visible. Only around **30,000 new international workers** joined social care directly from abroad in 2025/26, the lowest figure in four years.

Put problems two and three together and you get a simple, uncomfortable equation:

**Fewer British workers + fewer new overseas workers = a pipeline that cannot sustain itself.**

The low vacancy rate is a lagging indicator. It reflects hiring decisions made under rules that no longer exist.

## Why pay is part of the story

Every recruitment problem eventually becomes a pay conversation.

In April 2026 the National Living Wage rose 6.7% to £12.71 an hour. But [The King's Fund](https://www.kingsfund.org.uk/insight-and-analysis/long-reads/social-care-360-workforce-carers) points to Skills for Care analysis showing care worker pay growth had begun to lag the wage floor, with more staff paid within 10p of the legal minimum.

When a supermarket down the road pays the same, a care role has to compete on something else: flexibility, purpose, progression, or respect. The sector usually wins on purpose. It often loses on everything else.

There is a policy response on the way. The government has set out plans for the first [Fair Pay Agreement in adult social care](/blog/social-care-fair-pay-agreement-care-workforce-pathway). But it will not take effect for some time, and recruitment can't wait.

## What this means if you employ care staff

Averages hide individual choices. Here is what the data suggests a registered manager or recruiter should focus on now.

**1. Recruit domestically as if international recruitment never existed.**
For many providers, it effectively doesn't anymore. Build your plan around local talent pools and assume that's the whole pipeline.

**2. Look at who you are not reaching.**
Skills for Care data shared at the 2025 Care Show put the male share of the workforce at [around 21%](https://tribepad.com/article/the-state-of-care-recruitment-2026/). Career changers from retail, hospitality and customer service bring transferable skills. Returners and people who want part-time hours around caring responsibilities are worth a deliberate look too.

**3. Treat retention as recruitment.**
Every experienced carer who stays is a vacancy you never have to advertise. Clear progression, fair rotas, and recognition cost less than a constant hiring cycle.

**4. Make your careers visible, not just your jobs.**
A job advert says "care worker, £12.71". A career path says "care worker today, senior care worker in 18 months, team leader after that." The [Care Workforce Pathway](/blog/social-care-fair-pay-agreement-care-workforce-pathway) now gives you a national framework to show exactly that.

## What this means if you work in care

If you are a care worker, the data carries a quieter message: **you are more valuable to the system than you have ever been.**

Providers can no longer easily recruit from abroad. Domestic supply is falling. Demand is rising. That combination gives experienced, qualified carers more choice about where they work and more leverage to ask for development, flexibility and progression.

Use it. Keep your training records up to date. Build a professional profile that shows your qualifications and experience clearly. The people who document their careers tend to be the people who get offered the next step.

## The bottom line

A 10-year low in vacancies is worth celebrating. It is not a sign that the workforce problem is solved.

The sector is growing more slowly, losing British workers, and hiring fewer people from overseas, all while needing hundreds of thousands more posts by 2040. Skills for Care's chief executive has said the sector needs a long-term, collective approach, and the charity is bringing partners together to build a 10-year plan to attract people into care.

The providers who thrive over the next decade won't be the ones who wait for that plan. They will be the ones who start building their own pipeline today.
