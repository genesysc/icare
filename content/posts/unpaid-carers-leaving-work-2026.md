Most workforce crises are announced. This one happens quietly, one resignation at a time.

Someone's mother has a fall. A partner is diagnosed. A disabled child's support package changes. For a while they juggle it: annual leave, unpaid days, calls in the car park between meetings.

Then they hand in their notice.

New polling by Opinium for Carers UK, published in September 2026, puts a number on it: **3.33 million people in the UK have given up work to care** for someone older, disabled or seriously ill. **1.1 million of them left in the past two years alone — more than 1,500 people a day.**

That's a 150% increase on the charity's 2019 figure of around 600 a day.

## What the research found

The headline numbers are worth reading slowly, as reported by [Care Home Professional](https://www.carehomeprofessional.com/thousands-quit-work-care-relatives/) and [Social Care Today](https://socialcare.today/2026/09/16/over-1500-people-a-day-leave-work-to-care-for-loved-ones/):

- **One in five UK employees (20%)** now combines paid work with unpaid caring, up from 15% in 2019.
- **59% of working carers** said caring had affected their employment: reduced hours, lower-paid work, missed opportunities or changing employer to keep caring.
- **79%** of UK adults named flexible working as the most important support, and **79%** named a supportive line manager or employer.
- **73%** supported five days of paid carer's leave.

Carers UK's earlier "Tipping Point" research found **45% of carers** would be more likely to stay in work with paid carer's leave, and **35%** said more affordable, accessible or reliable social care would have helped them stay.

That last figure is the one the care sector should sit with. **A third of people who left work say better social care would have kept them in it.**

## Why this is an economic story, not just a personal one

Every carer who leaves work takes three things with them:

1. **Their skills**, often mid-career and hard to replace.
2. **Their income**, with knock-on effects for pensions and long-term financial resilience, a point [made by Standard Life](https://socialcare.today/2026/09/16/over-1500-people-a-day-leave-work-to-care-for-loved-ones/), which supported the research.
3. **Their tax contribution**, replaced in many cases by demand on public support.

For employers, this is the most preventable form of turnover there is. The person doesn't want to leave. They can't see how to stay.

## The policy picture right now

The rules are in the middle of changing, and it pays to be precise about where they stand.

- The existing statutory carer's leave entitlement is **unpaid**: up to one week in a 12-month period.
- The government launched a consultation on employment rights for unpaid carers and parents of seriously ill children on 9 June 2026. It **closed on 1 September 2026**.
- Officials are examining models of **paid** carer's leave alongside possible extensions to unpaid leave. The review is due to conclude in **winter 2026 to 2027**, and, as [HR Review notes](https://hrreview.co.uk/hr-news/jobs-labour-market/more-than-1500-people-a-day-leave-work-to-provide-unpaid-care/390497), it does not presuppose the current entitlement will change.
- Wider flexible working reforms under the Employment Rights Act are intended to make flexible-working requests more likely to be accepted.

So: **paid carer's leave is under consideration, not in force.** Anyone telling staff otherwise is getting ahead of the law.

## What employers can do now, without waiting for legislation

Most of what keeps carers in work costs less than replacing them.

**1. Find out who your carers are.** Many never tell anyone. A simple, confidential way to flag caring responsibilities, plus managers who ask the question kindly, surfaces the problem before the resignation does.

**2. Write a carer's policy and mean it.** Set out leave, flexibility, emergency arrangements and who to talk to. Publish it. A policy nobody knows about protects nobody.

**3. Train line managers.** Carers named a supportive manager as being as important as flexible working itself. This is a management-capability issue as much as an HR one.

**4. Be genuinely flexible about patterns, not just hours.** Care rarely fits a neat schedule. Shift swaps, compressed weeks, predictable rotas and short-notice arrangements matter more than a blanket policy.

**5. Offer a return route.** If someone has to step away, keep the door open with a keep-in-touch arrangement. Re-recruiting someone who already knows your organisation beats starting cold.

## Why the care sector should care twice over

Health and social care employers are affected from both directions.

**As employers**, they have one of the most caring-heavy workforces in the economy. People drawn to care work are often the same people supporting family members at home.

**As providers**, they are part of the solution. When home care is affordable, reliable and available, working carers can stay in their jobs. When it isn't, they resign. That's the link between a [thin care workforce](/blog/social-care-vacancy-rate-2026) and lost employment across every other industry.

It is also why Carers UK argues the [Casey Commission](/blog/casey-commission-big-conversation-on-care) must recognise the role a functioning care system plays in supporting unpaid carers. The Big Conversation on Care is open until April 2027, and carers are one of the groups whose evidence is most directly relevant.

## What to do if you're a working carer

**Tell your employer sooner than feels comfortable.** Most support is easier to arrange before a crisis.

**Know your current rights**: unpaid carer's leave, the right to request flexible working, and any additional support in your employer's own policy.

**Get a carer's assessment from your local authority.** It is the route to practical support, and many carers never request one.

**Think twice before resigning outright.** Reduced hours, a career break or a role change may protect your income, pension and route back better than leaving entirely.

## The bottom line

Britain has a hidden workforce: 1.1 million people who left a job in two years because caring and working became impossible to combine.

Fixing that isn't only about generosity to carers. It's about keeping skilled people in employment and recognising that the availability of good social care is an economic policy, not just a social one.

Employers can act now. Policy may follow this winter. The carers making the decision this month can't wait for either.
