Ask a care worker what their job is, and you'll usually get a clear answer.

Ask where their career is going, and the answer often gets vaguer.

That gap between *job* and *career* sits at the centre of social care's recruitment problem. People don't just leave roles because of pay. They leave because they can't see a next step, or because the next step elsewhere pays the same with less stress.

On 16 July 2026, the government published two things designed to close that gap at the same time: the details of the first **Fair Pay Agreement** for adult social care, and **Part 3 of the Care Workforce Pathway**.

One tackles pay and conditions. The other tackles progression. Together, they are the most significant structural change to care work in years.

Here's what each one does, when it lands, and what you can do about it now.

## Part 1: the Fair Pay Agreement

### What it is

A Fair Pay Agreement is a legally ratified set of minimum standards for pay, terms and conditions, training and career progression across a whole sector.

For adult social care in England, it will be negotiated by a new **Adult Social Care Negotiating Body**, bringing together worker representatives (trade unions) and employer representatives, overseen by an independent chair and operating at arm's length from ministers. The powers come from the Employment Rights Act 2025.

In its [written statement](https://questions-statements.parliament.uk/written-statements/detail/2026-07-16/hcws281), the government said the agreement will be backed by **£500 million in 2028/29** and will introduce annual negotiations on pay and conditions for the first time.

### When it happens

This is where patience comes in. The process runs over several years:

1. **Autumn 2026:** the government said it would appoint a chair in the autumn, with secondary legislation to formally establish the negotiating body.
2. **2027:** negotiations begin. The [RCN's timeline](https://www.rcn.org.uk/magazines/Action/2026/Aug/Adult-social-care-Fair-Pay-Agreement-explained) points to April 2027.
3. **April 2028:** the RCN expects the first Fair Pay Agreement to take effect in covered workers' contracts.

The consultation that shaped the design received 1,077 standard responses, according to the [government's response](https://www.gov.uk/government/consultations/fair-pay-agreement-process-in-adult-social-care/outcome/fair-pay-agreement-process-in-adult-social-care-government-response).

### Why it matters

For decades, care pay has been set provider by provider, contract by contract, often squeezed between local authority fee rates and the National Living Wage. There was no sector-wide table where workers and employers negotiated together.

A Fair Pay Agreement creates that table.

It won't fix everything. Funding levels, how councils commission care, and how the agreement is enforced will all matter. Scotland and Wales are developing their own approaches, and Northern Ireland isn't covered by these powers. But for England, it is a genuine shift from "whatever the market bears" to a negotiated floor.

## Part 2: the Care Workforce Pathway

### What it is

The Care Workforce Pathway is England's first universal career structure for adult social care. It sets out the knowledge, skills, values and behaviours expected in different roles, and how people can move between them.

Part 3, published in July 2026, added **ten new role categories**. According to the [GOV.UK overview](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/care-workforce-pathway-for-adult-social-care/care-workforce-pathway-for-adult-social-care-overview), the expansion brings in people outside direct care, including managers, learning and development practitioners, and supporting roles like catering and maintenance.

In a [joint DHSC blog](https://socialcare.blog.gov.uk/2026/07/17/a-fair-deal-for-care-workers-launching-part-3-of-the-care-workforce-pathway/), England's chief nurse for adult social care, the chief nursing officer and Skills for Care's chief executive described it as a single career structure reflecting a workforce of around 1.5 million people, with routes into nursing, social work and leadership.

### What it isn't

It isn't mandatory. It isn't a new CQC rule. Providers can adopt as much or as little of it as suits them.

That flexibility is sensible. It also means the pathway only helps workers if employers actually use it.

### The qualifications that sit alongside it

The pathway connects to real, funded qualifications:

- **Level 2 Adult Social Care Certificate**: an Ofqual-regulated qualification that sets a baseline standard and reduces the need to repeat training when staff move employer.
- **Learning and Development Support Scheme (LDSS)**: backed by up to £10 million in 2026/27. Eligible employers can claim funding for eligible courses and qualifications, including the Level 2 certificate, completed by the end of February 2027.
- Higher-level diplomas and apprenticeships continue to support progression into senior, specialist and management roles.

## The big idea: portability

If you only remember one word from all of this, make it **portability**.

For years, a carer who changed employer often had to re-do training, re-prove competence and start again at the bottom of a new pay scale. Every move reset the clock.

A national pathway and a regulated baseline qualification start to change that. Your skills become recognisable across employers. Your progress travels with you.

That's good for workers. It is also good for providers, who spend less time and money re-training experienced staff, at a moment when [domestic recruitment has to carry the sector](/blog/social-care-vacancy-rate-2026).

## What care workers should do now

**1. Find your place on the pathway.**
Look at the role categories and work out where you sit today and where you want to be in two years.

**2. Ask about the Level 2 certificate.**
If you've completed induction and have some experience, ask your manager whether they will claim LDSS funding for it. The funding window for 2026/27 is time-limited.

**3. Keep a record of everything.**
Certificates, training dates, specialisms, supervision notes. When a Fair Pay Agreement eventually links pay and progression more closely, documented experience will matter.

**4. Have your say.**
Trade unions will gather members' views before negotiations start in 2027. That's the moment worker input carries the most weight.

## What providers should do now

**1. Map your roles to the pathway.**
You don't need to rename every job. Compare what you have against the framework and look for the gaps.

**2. Claim the funding.**
LDSS money that isn't claimed isn't spent on your staff. Build a training plan that uses it before February 2027.

**3. Advertise careers, not vacancies.**
Candidates respond to visible progression. Show the step after the one you're hiring for.

**4. Plan for 2028.**
A negotiated pay floor will affect budgets and commissioning. Providers who model it early will be better placed than those who wait, especially those that can no longer [recruit care workers from overseas](/blog/health-care-worker-visa-settlement-2026).

## The bottom line

Social care has long asked people to treat caring as a vocation while offering few of the structures that make any profession feel like a career.

The Fair Pay Agreement and the Care Workforce Pathway won't change that overnight. The first agreement is still around two years away, and adoption of the pathway is voluntary.

But the direction is clear. Care work is being rebuilt as a profession with standards, progression and a seat at the negotiating table. The workers and employers who start building on that foundation now will be ahead when it's finished.
