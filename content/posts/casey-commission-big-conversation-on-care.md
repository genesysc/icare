Social care reform in England has a familiar rhythm.

A crisis. A promise. A review. A quiet retreat.

Baroness Casey named that cycle herself when she launched the Big Conversation on Care in July 2026, warning that past attempts at reform failed because they tried to settle fundamental questions without first building public understanding and consent.

This time, two things are different. The timetable has been pulled forward by a year. And the public, including the people who do the work, are being asked directly what the system should look like.

If you work in care, this is the closest thing to a say you have had in a long time.

## What changed in July

On 29 July 2026, the Prime Minister set out a new approach to adult social care, and Baroness Casey [launched the Big Conversation on Care](https://caseycommission.co.uk/2026/07/demo-news-article-in-conversation/) at an event in North London alongside the Prime Minister and the health and social care secretary.

Three changes matter:

**1. The Commission will report a year early.** The Independent Commission on Adult Social Care was originally due to deliver its second phase by 2028. Baroness Casey has now agreed to provide recommendations by **summer 2027**, with a plan for a National Care Service.

**2. The public gets a formal role.** The Big Conversation runs until **April 2027** and aims to reach a million people.

**3. Reform starts with the workforce.** The government said the health and social care secretary will chair a new ministerial group on workforce, system reform and delivery, as [Care Home Professional reported](https://www.carehomeprofessional.com/big-conversation-public-appeal/).

That third point deserves attention. Workforce is usually the last chapter of a reform plan. Putting it first is a different kind of statement.

## Why acceleration matters

Before the change, the maths didn't work.

A commission that reported in 2028 would hand its recommendations to a government heading towards a general election. MPs on the Health and Social Care Committee [examined exactly this problem](https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/cm5902/cmselect/cmhealth/420/report.html) in mid-2026, and heard that Baroness Casey believed she could expedite publication.

Reporting in summer 2027 leaves time for decisions to be made rather than merely debated.

It does not, however, change the hard part. Reform still needs money, and the sector has been here before. As one social care leader put it after the announcement: attention is only the first step.

## What the Big Conversation is actually asking

This is not a tick-box survey. It's structured in two phases and built around the questions politicians usually avoid:

**Phase one (July to November 2026)** asks about the values and principles people expect from a care system, and **who should be eligible for support**.

**Phase two** asks **how care should be funded** and how reform should be delivered.

Alongside the open online platform, a nationally representative **Public Panel on Care**, chosen at random, will work through the trade-offs together and feed conclusions directly into the Commission's recommendations.

The Commission's July newsletter reported [more than 70,000 votes](https://caseycommission.co.uk/2026/07/july-2026-newsletter-the-big-conversation-on-care-is-now-live/) on the platform within days of launch.

## The questions care workers should want answered

Ask ten people in the sector what "National Care Service" means and you'll get ten answers. That vagueness is a risk. Here are the questions worth pressing on while the conversation is open.

**Who pays, and for whom?** Eligibility is the sharpest question in social care. Widening it costs money. Keeping it narrow leaves families to fill the gap.

**Where does the workforce sit?** If care is to work alongside the NHS, what does that mean for pay parity, terms and conditions, training and portability of skills between the two systems?

**What happens to providers?** Most care in England is delivered by independent providers, from national groups to single-site homes and small domiciliary agencies. A national service has to work through them, not around them.

**How does this connect to the reforms already in motion?** A [Fair Pay Agreement is being negotiated from 2027](/blog/social-care-fair-pay-agreement-care-workforce-pathway), the Care Workforce Pathway has just expanded, and [international recruitment has been cut off](/blog/health-care-worker-visa-settlement-2026). The Commission's recommendations will land on top of all of it.

## What to do now if you work in care

**Take part, and take your team with you.** The platform is open to anyone in England. Fifteen minutes from a registered manager, a care worker and a nurse is worth more than a hundred pages of speculation about what the sector thinks.

**Bring specifics, not slogans.** "We need more funding" is easy to nod at and easy to ignore. "Our rota breaks because two people leave every quarter for warehouse work at the same hourly rate" is the kind of detail that changes minds.

**Say what good looks like.** The first phase is about values. If you want continuity of carer, time to care rather than 15-minute visits, and a career structure that pays for experience, say so now.

## What to do now if you run a service

**Prepare for change in both directions.** A National Care Service could reshape commissioning, contracts and quality expectations. Providers who can show outcomes data, stable staffing and strong training records will be better placed under any model.

**Use the reforms already here.** The Care Workforce Pathway, the Level 2 Adult Social Care Certificate and Learning and Development Support Scheme funding all exist today. Adoption isn't a bet on the Commission's conclusions. It's good practice regardless.

**Make the workforce case loudly.** With ministers explicitly starting with workforce, the sector's evidence about recruitment, retention and pay has an unusually short route to policy.

## The honest caveat

None of this is delivery. It is a timetable, a conversation and a promise.

The Commission's recommendations arrive in summer 2027. Legislation, funding and implementation come after that. Meanwhile, providers are running services today with a shrinking domestic workforce and no overseas recruitment route for care worker roles.

Reform and the day job are running on different clocks. Both need attention.

## The bottom line

For once, the sector isn't being asked to wait quietly while a review happens somewhere else.

The Big Conversation is open until April 2027. The Commission reports in summer 2027. And the government has said it wants to start with the workforce.

The people who know what's broken are the people doing the work. The next twelve months are the window in which saying so counts.
