Imagine spending three years training for a job the country says it desperately needs.

You pay £9,535 a year in tuition. You work thousands of unpaid placement hours. You pass every assessment. You get your registration.

Then you open the job boards and find almost nothing you are allowed to apply for.

This is not a hypothetical. It is the situation facing thousands of newly qualified nurses, physiotherapists and other clinicians in 2026. And it exists alongside a health service that still reports staff shortages on wards and waiting lists that stretch for months.

Two things can be true at once. The NHS can be short of staff *and* unable to hire the staff it trained. Understanding why is the first step to navigating it.

## The numbers behind the nursing jobs gap

In late August 2026, the Royal College of Nursing's students committee wrote to NHS England with the results of an audit of nursing adverts from July. The findings, reported by [Nursing Times](https://www.nursingtimes.net/newly-registered-nurses/rcn-writes-to-nhs-leaders-over-ongoing-lack-of-new-graduate-jobs-28-08-2026/), were stark:

- **33%** of band 5 vacancies required experience that ruled out new graduates.
- Only **26%** of adverts in England (82 out of 317) were full-time, permanent and open to applicants without professional experience.
- In the East of England and Yorkshire and the Humber, that fell to **13%**, just four out of 30 jobs.

A separate RCN Freedom of Information request found that advertised band 5 nursing and midwifery posts in London fell by 68% between 2019-20 and 2024-25.

Wales tells a similar story. When Health Education and Improvement Wales announced 809 roles for nurses qualifying in 2026, the [RCN calculated](https://www.rcn.org.uk/news-and-events/Press-Releases/rcn-wales-responds-to-new-heiw-streamlining-figures-110526) that only 69% of Wales-trained nurses would have an NHS Wales job.

## It isn't only nurses

Physiotherapy graduates are facing the same wall.

The Chartered Society of Physiotherapy has reported that physio graduates in 2024 were [significantly more likely to still be job-hunting](https://www.csp.org.uk/news/2025-06-25-csp-takes-action-address-graduate-recruitment-concerns) three to six months after qualifying than those in 2022. Nearly half of its workforce reps reported recruitment freezes or delays, and one in ten members said their organisation had frozen clinical recruitment entirely.

This is happening while the profession grows. More than 3,000 physiotherapists were expected to graduate in 2025, the highest number in a decade. In March 2026 the CSP called for a [graduate employment guarantee](https://www.csp.org.uk/news/2026-03-16-csp-responds-healthwatch-survey-nhs-waiting-times) for newly qualified physios.

## Why shortages and unemployment can exist together

It feels like a contradiction. It isn't. It's a gap between **need** and **funded posts**.

A ward can need two more nurses. But if the trust's budget doesn't cover those salaries, the job is never advertised. The need is real. The vacancy is not.

Three forces widen that gap:

**1. Financial pressure.** When budgets tighten, recruitment freezes are one of the quickest levers a trust can pull. They are also one of the most damaging, because the people locked out first are the newest.

**2. Experience requirements.** When a manager can only hire one person, they often pick the one who needs the least support. Asking for experience feels safe. At system level, it means nobody gets the experience in the first place.

**3. Internal and local adverts.** Some roles are filled through internal lists or local streamlining schemes before they are ever posted publicly. Graduates outside those networks never see them.

None of these decisions is irrational on its own. Added together, they create a pipeline that trains people and then drops them.

## What the Graduate Guarantee did and didn't do

In August 2025, the government launched a Graduate Guarantee encouraging NHS employers in England to support newly qualified nurses and midwives into posts.

A year on, the RCN says the guarantee has fallen short of what was promised. Some graduates have been [placed on job waiting lists](https://www.nursinginpractice.com/latest-news/newly-registered-nurses-put-on-waiting-lists-for-employment-report-reveals/) rather than into roles.

There have been other moves. In February 2026 the government announced that pay for band 5 Agenda for Change staff would rise ahead of wider contract talks, which the [CSP welcomed](https://www.csp.org.uk/news/2026-02-11-csp-welcomes-starting-salary-increase-questions-funding) while questioning how it would be funded.

But a higher starting salary doesn't help if the starting job doesn't exist.

## A practical playbook for newly qualified clinicians

You can't fix NHS finances. You can improve your odds. Here's what tends to work.

**Widen the map, not just the search radius.**
Most graduates think in hospitals. But registered nurses and allied health professionals are employed across primary care, community services, hospices, mental health, adult social care and [independent healthcare](/blog/careers-in-independent-healthcare-uk). Some of those employers are still struggling to fill posts.

**Treat your placements as evidence.**
If adverts ask for experience, show them the experience you have. Be specific: setting, dates, caseload, skills. The CSP advises graduates to spell out placement settings and responsibilities clearly on every application.

**Build a professional profile before you need it.**
Recruiters look for proof that you are ready: registration status, mandatory training, placements, references, and the specialisms you are drawn to. Keeping all of that in one place makes every application faster and stronger.

**Stay close to your network.**
Mentors, practice educators and former placement teams often hear about posts before they are advertised. A short message asking to be kept in mind costs nothing.

**Don't disappear from the profession.**
If you take a job outside your field while you search, keep your CPD active and your registration current. Gaps are easier to explain when you've stayed connected.

## What employers can do

If you hire clinicians, ask yourself one question: **who gets the experience if nobody hires people without it?**

Structured preceptorship, rotational band 5 programmes, and adverts that genuinely welcome newly qualified applicants are how the next generation of senior staff gets built. Investing in graduates during a freeze is also how organisations prepare for whatever the long-awaited [NHS 10 Year Workforce Plan](/blog/nhs-10-year-workforce-plan-delay) asks of them.

## The bottom line

The UK is not short of people who want to be nurses and physiotherapists. It is short of funded posts that let them start.

Until that changes, newly qualified clinicians need to think like job-seekers in a tight market: look wider, document everything, and build a visible professional identity that travels with them from their first role to their last.

The skills are there. The profession needs them. The job is to make sure they don't get lost in the gap.
