Ask a final-year nursing student where they'll work after graduation, and most will name a hospital trust.

Ask a physiotherapy student, a radiographer or an operating department practitioner, and you'll probably hear the same.

Very few say "an independent healthcare provider." And that's a blind spot worth fixing, because a large share of the care the NHS pays for isn't delivered by the NHS at all.

## The part of the system hiding in plain sight

In May 2026, the Independent Healthcare Providers Network (IHPN) published its [Workforce Report 2026](https://www.ihpn.org.uk/workforce-report-2026-introduction/). The figures on the sector's reach are striking. Independent providers now deliver:

- around **one in five** NHS elective operations,
- around **one in four** NHS diagnostic tests and scans,
- and make up **40%** of NHS community providers.

On top of that, private hospital admissions are close to one million a year, and IHPN's research suggests more than seven in ten people would now consider paying for treatment.

Behind all of that activity is a workforce of more than **150,000 people**.

Yet, as IHPN puts it, that workforce is often overlooked, both as a career destination for healthcare graduates and in national workforce planning.

## What staff say it's actually like

Statistics describe the size of a sector. They don't tell you what Monday morning feels like.

IHPN's report drew on focus groups, interviews and surveys across clinical, operational and non-clinical roles. [LaingBuisson News](https://www.laingbuissonnews.com/healthcare-markets-content/news-healthcare-markets-content/ihpn-publishes-workforce-report/) summarised the findings as three Ps: **patients, progression and pace**.

### Patients

Staff highlighted the chance to provide more personalised, unhurried care. For many clinicians, time with patients is the reason they entered healthcare, and the thing they feel squeezed out of elsewhere.

### Progression

This came through as a core strength. According to [IHPN's progression findings](https://www.ihpn.org.uk/workforce-report-2026/), many staff said development opportunities were a main reason they joined. They described employers that:

- fund external courses, postgraduate qualifications and specialist training,
- create new roles around people's skills without long bureaucratic processes,
- recognise initiative with quicker progression,
- and value skills from non-health backgrounds, including customer service and retail.

That last point is worth underlining. If you're changing career into healthcare, the independent sector may be more open to your background than you expect.

### Pace

Staff described being able to trial new ideas and put improvements in place without layers of sign-off. For people who like to fix things, that autonomy is a big draw.

## An honest look at the trade-offs

No sector is perfect, and a useful career guide should say so.

IHPN's own report notes that workloads and stress levels remain intense, even where support from managers is strong. Terms and conditions vary more between independent employers than within the NHS, where Agenda for Change sets a common framework. Pension arrangements, sick pay and study leave can differ significantly.

None of that is a reason to avoid the sector. It is a reason to read contracts carefully and ask direct questions at interview.

## A training ground, too

One common assumption is that independent providers only hire people the NHS has already trained.

That isn't the whole picture. IHPN's [In Train research](https://www.ihpn.org.uk/topics/workforce/) found that more than 15,000 people a year are trained in independent healthcare settings, including around 5,000 nurses.

IHPN has also evaluated apprenticeship delivery in the sector and published principles for the assurance of resident doctors working in independent settings.

## Why this matters right now

Timing makes this conversation more urgent.

As we covered in our piece on [newly qualified nurses and physios struggling to find jobs](/blog/newly-qualified-nurses-physios-no-jobs-2026), NHS recruitment freezes are locking some graduates out of their first posts. The Chartered Society of Physiotherapy has noted that, [elsewhere in the NHS and in the independent sector](https://www.csp.org.uk/frontline/article/we-cant-afford-leave-talent-sidelines), employers are still struggling to fill vacancies.

In other words, while some doors are closing, others are open. Graduates who only look at one part of the system may be missing the part that is hiring.

## How to explore a career in independent healthcare

**1. Map the providers near you.**
Private hospitals, diagnostic centres, day surgery units, community service providers, mental health providers and clinics all count. Many deliver NHS contracts.

**2. Ask what proportion of their work is NHS-funded.**
It tells you a lot about case mix, stability and the kind of patients you'll see.

**3. Compare the whole package, not just salary.**
Pension, sick pay, annual leave, study budget and supervision can matter more over five years than a small difference in base pay.

**4. Ask about progression in specifics.**
"What did the last person in this role move on to?" is one of the most revealing questions you can ask at interview.

**5. Keep your options open both ways.**
Plenty of clinicians move between NHS and independent roles over a career. Experience in both is increasingly common, and increasingly valued.

## What independent employers can learn from this

If you recruit for an independent provider, IHPN's findings hand you a recruitment message on a plate: patients, progression and pace.

The challenge is visibility. Graduates don't consider employers they've never heard of. Build relationships with universities, offer placements where you can, and show real career stories, not just vacancy lists.

## The bottom line

The UK's healthcare system is bigger than any single employer, and as the [delayed NHS 10 Year Workforce Plan](/blog/nhs-10-year-workforce-plan-delay) shows, national planning doesn't always move at the speed careers do. Independent providers deliver a significant share of NHS care, train thousands of professionals a year, and employ more than 150,000 people.

For healthcare professionals, especially those starting out or feeling stuck, that makes the independent sector worth a serious look. The best careers are often built by people who see the whole map, not just the most familiar route.
