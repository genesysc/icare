In September 2026, around a thousand women a year in England gained access to a treatment that can extend their lives.

NICE recommended Enhertu for routine NHS use in HER2-low advanced breast cancer, reversing an earlier rejection after a commercial agreement and changes to how NICE assesses value.

It's genuinely good news. Charities called it a momentous day.

And then, quietly, the harder question arrives: **an approval is a permission, not a delivery.** Someone has to prescribe it, prepare it, administer it, monitor it and support the patient through it.

That someone is a workforce already under strain.

## What changed at NICE

Two technical changes have unlocked a run of decisions.

**The threshold went up.** In April 2026, NICE's cost-effectiveness threshold rose to **£25,000–£35,000 per quality-adjusted life year**, the first increase since it was introduced in the early 2000s. The [ABPI's 2026 competitiveness report](https://www.abpi.org.uk/publications/uk-pharmaceutical-investment-competitiveness-report-2026/) records that nine additional medicines were approved in the three months that followed, covering conditions including blood disorders, autoimmune disease and cancers.

**The method changed.** A new approach to assessing quality-of-life benefits was published on **27 August 2026**, which allowed NICE to revisit previously rejected treatments, as [National Health Executive reported](https://www.nationalhealthexecutive.com/articles/enhertu-approved-routine-nhs-use-major-breakthrough-her2-low-breast-cancer-patients).

Enhertu was one of the first beneficiaries. [Nursing Times reported](https://www.nursingtimes.net/cancer/nice-approves-life-extending-treatment-for-advanced-breast-cancer-22-09-2026/) that the change of heart followed a deal with the manufacturers after protocol changes linked to the UK–US pharmaceutical pricing arrangement.

Fast-track pilots aimed at getting approved medicines to eligible patients sooner, particularly for rare and life-limiting conditions, began testing from September.

The direction is clear: **more medicines, approved faster.**

## The capacity question nobody puts in the press release

Modern cancer medicines are rarely a tablet handed over at a counter. Many are infusions or injections that require:

- a prescriber and a clinic slot,
- pharmacy **aseptic preparation** in a regulated facility,
- a nurse and a chair to administer them,
- monitoring, toxicity management and follow-up.

Each of those is a workforce input. And the evidence says each is tight.

A [2026 national evaluation](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42496203/) of how UK services have implemented national guidance on managing aseptic capacity pressures in systemic anti-cancer therapy found implementation was selective and variable, with **workforce shortages, infrastructure limits and governance complexity** among the key barriers. Its conclusion was blunt: national guidance alone is not enough without coordinated implementation support and system-level workforce investment.

On the nursing side, a [House of Commons Library briefing](https://commonslibrary.parliament.uk/research-briefings/cdp-2025-0186/) cited analysis suggesting that a third of acute trusts in England were missing at least 10% of their planned nurses across haematology and oncology wards, based on monthly data from January 2023 to November 2024. The same briefing noted a Royal College of Radiologists survey in which 56% of clinical directors did not believe there were enough radiologists to deliver safe and effective care.

So the pipeline looks like this: more approvals at the front, constrained capacity in the middle, patients waiting at the end.

## Why this matters beyond cancer

Cancer is the clearest example, but the pattern is general.

Every new treatment that reaches NHS patients creates downstream work: clinics, monitoring, pharmacy preparation, diagnostics to identify eligible patients, and specialist nursing to support them.

Advances in genomics and targeted therapy make this sharper, because eligibility often depends on testing first. More precise medicine means more diagnostic and laboratory work, not less.

Access policy and workforce policy are the same policy. They just get announced separately.

## What this means for careers

If you're deciding where to specialise, follow the demand.

**Cancer and specialist nursing.** SACT delivery, acute oncology and clinical nurse specialist roles sit at the centre of expanding treatment pathways.

**Pharmacy, especially aseptic and cancer services.** Aseptic preparation is safety-critical, regulated and short-staffed. Technicians, aseptic services staff and cancer pharmacists are in demand, and [prescribing rights now arrive with registration](/blog/pharmacist-independent-prescribers-2026).

**Diagnostics and imaging.** Radiographers, biomedical scientists and pathology staff are the gatekeepers of targeted treatment. No test, no eligibility.

**Clinical research.** More medicines means more trials. The [UK's clinical research workforce](/blog/uk-pharmaceutical-industry-jobs-skills-2026) supports tens of thousands of jobs and needs research nurses, coordinators and data specialists.

These are not fringe niches. They are where treatment expansion meets staffing reality.

## What this means for employers and system leaders

**Plan capacity alongside approvals.** When a new treatment is recommended for a cohort you serve, model the pharmacy, nursing and chair time it needs before the first referral arrives, not after the waiting list forms.

**Grow your own specialists.** Aseptic and SACT skills are learned on the job. Rotational posts, apprenticeships and structured training convert general staff into the specialists you cannot recruit off the shelf.

**Protect the enabling roles.** Pharmacy technicians, assistants and administrators are often first in line when budgets tighten, and they are precisely the people who free clinicians to treat.

## The bottom line

A medicine approval is a promise that only the workforce can keep.

The UK has spent 2026 making it easier for new treatments to reach patients: a higher NICE threshold, a new valuation method, fast-track pilots and a pricing arrangement that unlocked stalled decisions.

The next test is whether cancer services, pharmacy and diagnostics have the people to turn those decisions into treatment.

For anyone choosing a clinical career right now, that's not just a policy problem. It's a signal about where the work will be.
