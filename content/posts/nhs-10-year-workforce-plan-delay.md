There is an old saying in project management: a plan is only as good as the people who deliver it.

The NHS in England has a plan. The [10 Year Health Plan](https://www.nhsemployers.org/news/understanding-workforce-implications-10-year-health-plan), published in July 2025, set out three big shifts: from hospital to community, from analogue to digital, and from treatment to prevention.

What it doesn't yet have is the companion document that says who will do all of that.

The **10 Year Workforce Plan** has now been delayed several times. For the more than a million people who work in the NHS in England, and the thousands more hoping to join, that delay isn't an abstract policy story. It shapes which jobs get funded, which training posts exist, and where careers are heading.

## A short history of a long wait

Here's how the timeline has slipped:

- **Late 2024:** the then health secretary announced the government would rework the NHS workforce plan.
- **Summer 2025:** original target for publication.
- **Spring 2026:** revised target, confirmed in [parliamentary answers](https://commonslibrary.parliament.uk/nhs-workforce/).
- **May 2026:** NHS England's deputy chief pharmaceutical officer told a conference it was [expected in June](https://pharmaceutical-journal.com/article/news/nhs-england-workforce-plan-expected-in-june-2026).
- **8 July 2026:** James Murray, health secretary at the time, told the Health and Social Care Committee the plan was "largely done" but [couldn't confirm a publication date](https://www.nursinginpractice.com/latest-news/health-secretary-pressed-on-nhs-workforce-plan-delays/).
- **August 2026:** [Nursing Times reported](https://www.nursingtimes.net/workforce/latest-workforce-news/australia-publishes-first-nursing-workforce-strategy-12-08-2026/) that publication was expected in autumn 2026.

The summer also brought a change in national political leadership, which DHSC officials had flagged as a possible factor in timing.

## Fewer staff, working differently

The biggest signal about the plan's direction came in the 10 Year Health Plan itself.

The 2023 Long Term Workforce Plan, produced under the previous government, projected a shortfall of [260,000 to 360,000 staff](https://www.kingsfund.org.uk/insight-and-analysis/long-reads/nhs-long-term-workforce-plan-explained) by 2036/37 without action and aimed for major growth in headcount.

The current government has taken a different view. According to the [House of Commons Library](https://commonslibrary.parliament.uk/nhs-workforce/), it has said there will be fewer NHS staff in 2035 than the 2023 plan projected, but that those staff will be better treated, better trained, and have more scope to develop.

The logic is that new models of care, a better mix of skills in teams, and technology will free up time.

It's a bold bet. And not everyone is convinced.

## Why unions asked for a pause

In June 2026, [GPonline reported](https://www.gponline.com/nhs-workforce-plan-based-false-assumptions-staffing-ai-health-unions-warn/article/1961192) that health organisations had urged the government to pause the plan, warning it risked falling significantly short of the workforce boost the health service needs.

Their concerns centred on two points:

1. **Scale.** The direction of the plan may not match the growth in staff that patient demand requires.
2. **AI.** The plan was likely to overstate the benefits of artificial intelligence without adequate evidence or safeguards.

This matters because a workforce plan built on optimistic productivity assumptions can lead to fewer training places and fewer funded posts. If the productivity gains don't arrive, the gap shows up later as vacancies, burnout and waiting lists.

## What we already know will be in it

While the full plan is still unpublished, the 10 Year Health Plan committed to several workforce changes. [NHS Employers](https://www.nhsemployers.org/news/understanding-workforce-implications-10-year-health-plan) summarised them, including:

- **New staff standards** from April 2026, with employer-level data published quarterly.
- **Reducing sickness absence** from 5.1%.
- **1,000 new specialty training posts** over three years, focused on specialties with the greatest need.
- **Reformed appraisals** and new ways to reward high-performing staff.
- **Reformed mandatory training** and new "skills escalators" to create clearer career progression.

And some signals from NHS leaders point to where careers are heading. Speaking in May, Richard Cattell highlighted neighbourhood working in integrated teams, digital skills, and the fact that more people want to work less or build portfolio careers.

## What this means for your career

You can't control when the plan is published. You can position yourself for the direction it is almost certainly going.

**Get comfortable outside the hospital.**
Community, primary care and neighbourhood teams are where the growth is designed to happen. Experience in those settings will likely carry more weight in the next decade.

**Build digital confidence.**
You don't need to become a data scientist. You do need to be comfortable with digital records, remote consultations and new tools. Employers are already signalling it.

**Think in skills, not just job titles.**
Skills escalators and new role types reward people who can show a clear record of what they can do. Keep your training, competencies and experience documented and up to date.

**Look across the whole system.**
The NHS doesn't deliver all NHS care. Independent providers deliver a significant share of NHS elective operations and diagnostics. We cover that in our guide to [careers in independent healthcare](/blog/careers-in-independent-healthcare-uk).

## What employers should do while they wait

Don't wait.

The [Health Foundation](https://www.health.org.uk/reports-and-analysis/analysis/getting-the-next-workforce-plan-right-eight-insights) has noted that past progress on staffing relied heavily on international recruitment, a lever that is now far more constrained. That means the organisations that grow their own talent, through apprenticeships, preceptorship and internal progression, will be more resilient regardless of what the national plan says.

And if you have [newly qualified clinicians knocking on your door](/blog/newly-qualified-nurses-physios-no-jobs-2026), this is the moment to find a way to let them in.

## The bottom line

The 10 Year Workforce Plan will eventually arrive. When it does, it will shape NHS careers for a decade.

But the direction is already visible: care closer to home, more digital work, more flexible careers, and a strong push to do more with the workforce the NHS already has.

The people and organisations who start moving in that direction now won't need to scramble when the document finally lands.
